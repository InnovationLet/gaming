When Unity announced a per-install fee in September 2023, studios that had shipped years of titles on the engine spent a week running spreadsheets to figure out what they'd owe. The policy got walked back, but the reaction showed how deeply an engine choice locks into a business.

## Sticker price versus hidden cost

Unreal Engine charges no subscription, then takes 5% of revenue once a game earns beyond $1 million. Unity bills per seat and per revenue tier, with fees that have changed more than once. In-house engines look free, until you count the salaries of the team that builds and maintains them.

That comparison is where studios get tripped up. A 5% royalty on a hit is a lot of money, but it's a problem you have after success. Paying ten engineers for three years to write a renderer is a bill you cover whether the game sells or not.

## What you trade for control

An in-house engine means you own the pipeline, the profiling tools, and every workaround you've ever needed. Nothing gets deprecated out from under you. The cost is hiring: fewer engineers have shipped with a proprietary engine, so onboarding takes months and turnover hurts more.

Off-the-shelf engines give you a large talent pool, marketplace plugins, and regular updates. The trade is dependency. When the licensor changes pricing, drops a feature, or shifts focus to a new platform, your roadmap bends around someone else's decisions.

## Portability and the second game

Engine choice compounds after the first project. Unreal's tooling makes moving to another console fairly mechanical, and its network layer saves months on multiplayer work. A well-built in-house engine makes the sequel cheap to produce, as long as the original team stays around and the code still builds.

The trap is building for the game you have instead of the studio you're becoming. An engine tuned for one sprawling open-world project can turn into dead weight if the next idea is a small, fast, 2D thing.

**Takeaway:** Prototype the same vertical slice in two engines before committing. Whichever one gets your team to a playable build faster, with people you can actually hire, is the one that'll cost you less over the next five years.
